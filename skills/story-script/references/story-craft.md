# Story craft: from a topic to a story worth hearing

Distilled from dramaturgy practice (premise, value turns, scene craft, openings and endings) and from producing
narrated animated videos. Works for fiction, history, biography and explainers.

## 1. Choose the material

- Pick what moved you. If it does not move you, it will not move a listener.
- **Small and single beats big and scattered.** One event told deeply is stronger than an era summarized. If you
  cannot tell the line of the story in one minute, it is too tangled.
- Look for the **kernel**: one situation that can grow, holds a conflict, and means something beyond itself.
- Check the facts before writing. Keep a fact list with a source per claim.

## 2. The premise rules everything

Answer five questions: **Who** is it about? What do they **want**? **Who or what** stands in the way? **Why now**?
What has **changed** by the end?

- One premise per video. Two premises pull the story in two directions.
- **Controlling idea = value + cause:** "X wins (or loses) because Y". Find it by deciding the climax first, then asking
  what value it settles and why.
- **Desire versus misbelief:** what the protagonist wants, and the false belief that blocks them. The plot is what
  happens; the story is how it changes the person. For an explainer, the misbelief is the audience's: what they think
  now versus what is true.
- The premise is never said aloud. Every scene proves it.

## 3. Find the spine, then cut

- One subject, one want, one change.
- Dramatize 3–5 moments where something changes; summarize everything between them in one line each.
- Budget: about one new place per minute and three or four named people per video.
- **Subtract** people and events that do not serve the kernel, even true and interesting ones. **Add** the motive behind
  any action that would otherwise look odd. **Multiply** one striking element instead of listing many. If the entry
  point is wrong, start again from another angle.

## 4. Structure

**Short (45–90 s):** hook (the paradox in the first sentence) → setting in one line → three or four escalating beats →
turn (the real cause, the "but") → payoff → a last line that lands the twist or calls back the opening.

**Long (3–10 min):** chapters work like sequences: two to five beats rising to a peak, each chapter with its own
question and title idea. The inciting event comes in the first tenth; a midpoint turn changes the direction; the
lowest point comes before the climax; the resolution is short.
- First part: **attraction**, make the listener want to follow.
- Middle: **anticipation**, let them guess what comes next, and sometimes guess wrong.
- End: **satisfaction**, pay off what was promised, but not in the expected way.

**Explainer variant:** a problem the listener feels → what people usually believe → the turn ("but") → how it really
works, in one to three concrete steps → what changes for the listener.

## 5. Scenes and beats

- **Every scene turns a value.** Note it at the start and at the end (+ or −). If they are the same, the scene is
  exposition: cut it and weave the information in elsewhere.
- Each scene does two jobs: new information, and more of the protagonist.
- **Enter late, leave early.** Leave before the conflict is fully resolved; the open end pulls into the next scene.
- **Why here, why now?** Put the scene where it adds pressure: a cook matters more in a desert camp where everyone is
  starving than in a kitchen.
- Something visible happens. Action beats talk, and in narrated animation the picture must have something to show.
- **A detail, not a concept.** "He liked to show off" is a concept. Meat stuck in his teeth that he picks at in public
  so everyone knows he ate well is a detail. Choose details that carry the time and place; show each one once unless
  repeating it moves the story.
- **A recurring object** can carry a whole story when its meaning changes each time it appears, each change caused by
  someone's action at a turning point.
- **Suspense:** raise questions, answer them, raise new ones. Letting the audience know more than the character
  (anticipation) usually beats keeping a secret (a surprise lasts one moment).
- **Delay before release:** crouch before the jump. Hold the payoff a beat longer than expected.
- **Changes make it alive:** relationships, fortunes, the situation and the mood should all shift several times. The
  best source of change is the character's own nature; coincidence and outside rescue are the weakest.

## 6. Openings and endings

- **Enter fast.** Start close to the conflict, with an action, a surprise or a strong image of the situation. Never
  start by explaining.
- **Know the ending before writing.** Endings fail when they are too neat, too long, too sudden or too soft.
- Ending shapes that work: the climax and the resolution in one stroke; an open question handed to the audience; an
  echo of the opening image or sound with a new meaning; a return to the opening situation, changed; one line that
  lights up everything before it.
- **The last image answers the first.**

## 7. Conflict and plausibility

- Conflict must be real (not staged), meaningful, and between wills, not just opinions.
- A strange event has to be believable first: strengthen the motives and the history behind it before using it.
- Do not preach. If the whole video reduces to a slogan, it has become a lecture; let the meaning come out of the
  events.
